    Worksheet 1.7. Gauss’ Law and the Divergence Theorem
    

    MTH 401
    🔗

    Theorem 20.

    Gauss’ Law. Consider an electric field \(\mbf E\) through a closed surface \(S\) which encloses the total charge \(Q\text{.}\) Then the electric flux out of \(S\) is equal to the total charge enclosed scaled by the permittivity constant, that is
    \begin{equation*} \ds \text{flux} = \oiint_S \mbf E \cdot ~d\mbf A = \oiint_S \mbf E \cdot \hat \nvec ~dA= \frac{Q}{\epsilon_0} \end{equation*}
    where \(\epsilon_0\) is the free space permittivity constant ( \(\epsilon_0\approx 8.854\times 10^{-12}\) Farads/meter) and \(\hat n\) is the outward normal vector.
    🔗
    The electric displacement, \(\mbf D\text{,}\) is given by \(\mbf D = \epsilon_0 \mbf E\text{,}\) thus equivalently
    \begin{equation*} \oiint_S \mbf D \cdot ~d\mbf A = \oiint_S \mbf D \cdot \hat \nvec ~dA = Q. \end{equation*}
    Using the divergence theorem where \(\rho\) is the total volume charge density (charge per unit volume), the differential form of Gauss’ Law is:
    \begin{equation*} \ds \nabla \cdot \mbf E = \frac{\rho}{\epsilon_0}\text{.} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    To find the electric potential \(\phi\), choose a path \(C\) from an arbitrary basepoint \(*\) to some point \((x,y)\text{.}\) Then
    \begin{equation*} \phi(x,y)= \work{C}{E}\text{.} \end{equation*}
    🔗

    1.

    Suppose that we have an infinitely thin charged plate whose charge density is 13 Coulombs per square meter as shown on the left below.
    🔗

    (a)

    What does the symmetry of this situation imply about the direction of the electric field and electric displacement vectors?
    🔗
    Solution.
    Rotational symmetry must hold. If there was any horizontal component to the field, it would not be symmetrical under rotation, so it must be completely vertical. Since the charge is positive, the field points away from the plate (up/down if you are above/below the plate).
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (b)

    Suppose we take a Gaussian surface that is a cylinder of radius \(r\) as shown on the right above. How much electric charge is contained within the cylinder? (Remember that all the charge is on the plate.)
    🔗
    Solution.
    Charge density is 13 Coulombs per square meter, so charge is the charge density times the area. The area with charge inside our Gaussian surface is just the disk within the plate that has area \(\pi r^2\text{,}\) so charge is \(13 \pi r^2\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (c)

    What does Gauss’ Law tell us about the strength of the electric displacement \(\mbf D\) at a position \(h\) meters above the plate? (What can you say about the flux of \(\mbf D\) through the curvy sides of the cylinder?)
    🔗
    Solution.
    \(\oiint_S \mbf D \cdot \nvec ~dA = charge = 13\pi r^2\text{.}\) Working on the left hand side: note on the sides of the cylinder, \(\mbf D\) is orthogonal to \(\nvec\text{,}\) so we only need to focus on the top and bottom circles of our Gaussian surface. Across those circles, \(\mbf D\) is a constant, and \(\nvec\) is a unit vector in the same direction, so \(\mbf D \cdot \nvec = |\mbf D| |\nvec| = |\mbf D|\text{.}\) So
    \begin{equation*} \oiint_S \mbf D \cdot \nvec ~dA = \iint_{2 \ circles} |\mbf D| ~dA = |\mbf D| (2\pi r^2). \end{equation*}
    Then plugging back in to Gauss’ Law: \(|\mbf D| (2\pi r^2) = 13 \pi r^2\text{.}\) Solving for the displacement we have \(|\mbf D| = \frac{13}2\text{.}\) (The strength.)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (d)

    Suppose that we want to create a potential \(\phi\) for the electric field \(\mbf E\) (remember that \(\mbf D = \epsilon_0 \mbf E\) ). Let’s choose a basepoint \(*\) on the plate. What is the value of the potential \(h\) meters above the plate?
    🔗
    Solution.
    Choose basepoint on the plate, and go straight up to some \(h\) meters above the plate. That’s our curve \(C\text{.}\) We can also think of the plate lying on the xy-plane, and so we go up to some distance \(z\) above the plane. Then \(\mbf D = \epsilon_0 \mbf E\text{,}\) and we know \(\mbf D\) from above, thus \(\mbf E = \frac{13}{2\epsilon_0} \widehat k\) above the plate since the direction is straight upwards in the \(\widehat k\) direction. (It would be \(- \frac{13}{2\epsilon_0} \widehat k\) below the plate). So,
    \begin{equation*} f = \int_C \mbf E \cdot d \mbf r = \int \frac{13}{2\epsilon_0} dz = \frac{13}{2\epsilon_0}z \end{equation*}
    above the plate. ( \(-\frac{13}{2\epsilon_0}z\) below the plate.). If we are sticking to the \(h\) in the problem (oops) it would be \(\frac{13}{2\epsilon_0} h\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    2.

    Suppose that we have an infinitely thin, infinitely long charged wire whose charge density is \(\lambda\) Coulombs per meter.
    🔗

    (a)

    Choose a cylindrical Gaussian surface of radius \(r\) and height \(h\) as shown below. How much electric charge is inside the surface?
    🔗
    Solution.
    We’ll say that cylinder has radius \(r\) and height \(h\text{.}\) Then the charge = (charge density) * (length of wire inside Gaussian surface) = \(\lambda h\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (b)

    What does the symmetry of the situation say about the direction of the electric field and electric displacement?
    🔗
    Solution.
    It must point radially away from the wire, away from the vertical axis.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (c)

    Use Gauss’ Law to find the electric displacement and electric field a distance \(r\) from the wire.
    🔗
    Solution.
    Since the field points radially outward, then \(\mbf D\cdot \nvec = \mbf E \cdot \nvec = 0\) on the top and bottom of the Gaussian surface. But they point in the same direction on the sides. Also, due to symmetry, \(|\mbf D|\) is the same everywhere on the side of the Gaussian surface, so Gauss’ Law simplifies as:
    \begin{align*} \oiint_S \mbf D \cdot \nvec~dA \amp = charge \ inside \ S\\ \iint_{sides} |\mbf D|~dA \amp = \lambda h \\ |\mbf D| \iint_{sides}~dA \amp = \lambda h \\ |\mbf D| 2\pi r h \amp = \lambda h \end{align*}
    which we can solve for \(|\mbf D| = \frac{\lambda}{2\pi r}\text{.}\) The electric field is the same but scaled by \(\frac1{\epsilon_0}\text{,}\) so \(|\mbf E| = \frac{\lambda}{2\pi\epsilon_0 r}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (d)

    Suppose we choose a basepoint \(1\) meter away from the wire. Find the electric potential \(r\) meters away from the wire.
    🔗
    Solution.
    The field is pointing in the outward radial direction, the same direction as \(d\mbf r\text{.}\) Thus \(\mbf E \cdot d\mbf r = |\mbf E| dr\) So we choose the basepoint 1 meter away from the wire and head straight horizontally out to some point \(r\) meters away from the wire, and that’s our curve \(C\text{.}\) So potential is
    \begin{equation*} f(r) = \int_C \mbf E \cdot d\mbf r = \int_C |\mbf E| dr = \int_{1}^r \frac{\lambda}{2\pi\epsilon_0 r} ~dr = \left. \frac{\lambda}{2\pi\epsilon_0}\ln(r)\right|_1^r = \frac{\lambda}{2\pi\epsilon_0}\ln(r)-0 \end{equation*}
    So \(f(r) = \frac{\lambda}{2\pi\epsilon_0}\ln(r)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    3.

    Suppose that we have a fluid whose velocity is given by the field \(\mbf F(x,y,z)\) meters per second. Suppose that the density of the fluid is \(\rho(x,y,z)\) kilograms per meter \(^3\text{.}\)
    🔗

    (a)

    If \(E\) is a three-dimensional region, what is the physical meaning of \(\iiint_E \rho~dV\text{?}\) (Thinking about units will help.)
    🔗
    Solution.
    Units: \(\rho\) is \(kg / m^3\text{,}\) and \(dV\) is a little bit of volume in units \(m^3\text{.}\) Then the product \(\rho ~dV\) is has units \((kg/m^3)(m^3) = kg\text{.}\) Then \(\iiint_E \rho ~dV \) is the total mass in kg inside E.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (b)

    Suppose that \(\mbf U = \rho \mbf F\text{.}\) What are the units on \(U\text{?}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    Again units, the field is velocity in units \(m/s\text{,}\) and so \(\mbf U=\rho \mbf F\) has units \((\frac{kg}{m^3})(\frac{m}{s}) = \frac{kg}{m^2s}\text{.}\) Less intuitive.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (c)

    If \(S\) is a surface, what is the physical meaning of \(\ds \iint_S \mbf U \cdot \hat\nvec~ dA\) if \(\hat \nvec\) is the outward normal to \(S\text{?}\) (Think units again...)
    🔗
    Solution.
    Units, there aren’t any for \(\nvec\text{.}\) But \(\mbf U \cdot \hat n~dA\) would have units \((\frac{kg}{m^2s})(m^2) = \frac{kg}{s}\) since \(dA\) would be an area in square meters. So \(\iint_S \mbf U \cdot\hat n~dA\) is the rate at which mass flows across the surface \(S\) in the direction \(\hat n\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (d)

    If \(S\) is a closed surface with outward normal \(\hat \nvec\) bounding a region \(E\text{,}\) explain why
    \begin{equation*} \ds \frac{d}{dt}\iiint_E \rho ~dV = -\oiint_S \mbf U\cdot\hat \nvec~dA, \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Solution.
    The left side is a partial derivative of the mass inside E, so it is the rate of change of mass inside E. Then \(\oiint_S \mbf U\cdot\hat \nvec~dA\) is the rate of mass flowing out of E through the surface S, and so \(-\oiint_S \mbf U\cdot\hat \nvec~dA\) is the rate of mass flowing into E through it’s surface S.
    🔗
    That is, the rate of change of mass in E is equal to the rate of mass flowing into our region E through the surface of the region S.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (e)

    Explain why \(\ds \frac{\partial \rho}{\partial t} + \nabla \cdot \mbf U = 0\text{.}\) This is called the continuity equation which describes the transport of mass and is an expression of conservation of mass.
    🔗
    Solution.
    By the Divergence theorem,
    \begin{equation*} \oiint_S\mbf U\cdot \hat n~dA = \iiint_E \nabla \cdot \mbf U~dV\text{.} \end{equation*}
    By the previous problem,
    \begin{equation*} \ds \frac{d}{dt}\iiint_E \rho ~dV = -\oiint_S \mbf U\cdot\hat \nvec~dA. \end{equation*}
    Well, there is no reason we can’t switch the order of the derivative and triple integral, since the density isn’t changing over time. So \(\ds \frac{d}{dt}\iiint_E \rho ~dV = \iiint_E \frac{\partial }{\partial t}(\rho) ~dV = \iiint_E \frac{\partial \rho}{\partial t}~dV\text{,}\) and so rearranging the previous problem and making this substitution along with substituting via the Divergence theorem, we have
    \begin{align*} \frac{d}{dt} \iiint_E \rho~dV + \oiint_S \mbf U \cdot \hat n ~dA \amp = 0 \\ \iiint_E \frac{\partial \rho}{\partial t}~dV + \iiint_E \nabla \cdot \mbf U ~dV \amp = 0 \\ \iiint_E \left(\frac{\partial \rho}{\partial t} + \nabla \cdot \mbf U \right)~dV \amp = 0 \end{align*}
    Since we made no assumptions on E, this is true for all 3-dimensional regions E, and so the only way to make this true is if
    \begin{equation*} \frac{\partial \rho}{\partial t} + \nabla \cdot \mbf U = 0. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    Fun fact: Continuity equations are stronger, local versions of conservation laws. For example, the Law of Conservation of Mass says that matter cannot be created or destroyed in an isolated system, that is, the total mass is conserved. But it does not say that matter cannot teleport! Matter that disappears from one area and reappears instantaneously in another area within the system would be completely acceptable according to the conservation law. The continuity equation says more: mass is locally conserved. Matter cannot be created nor destroyed, and it also cannot teleport. It can only move through a continuous flow. The way that mass flows is described by its flux. (Other quantities with continuity equations include energy, momentum, electric charge, ...)
