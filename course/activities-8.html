    Worksheet 1.5. Green’s Theorem
    

    MTH 401
    🔗

    Theorem 17.

    Green’s Theorem: For a two-dimensional field \(\mbf F = (P,Q)\) and a closed curve \(C\) that is oriented counterclockwise (positively oriented) and that encloses the region \(D\text{,}\) the work done by \(\mbf F\) along \(C\) is
    \begin{equation*} \ds \oint_C \mbf F \cdot ~d\mbf r = \iint_D \left(\frac{\partial Q}{\partial x} - \frac{\partial P}{\partial y}\right)~dA = \iint_D \nabla \times \mbf F~dA\text{.} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗

    1.

    Use Green’s Theorem to evaluate the line integral \(\ds \oint_C -y~dx + x~dy\) where \(C\) is the unit circle oriented in the counterclockwise direction.
    🔗
    Solution.
    We have \(P=-y, \ Q = x\text{,}\) so \(\frac{\partial P}{\partial y} = -1\) and \(\frac{\partial Q}{\partial x} = 1\text{,}\) so \(\frac{\partial Q}{\partial x}-\frac{\partial P}{\partial y}=1-(-1)=2\)
    \begin{equation*} \oint_C -y~dx + x~dy = \iint_D 2dA = 2(area \ of \ D) = 2\pi. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    2.

    Evaluate the line integral \(\ds \oint_C y^2~dx + 3xy~dy\) where \(C\) is the curve shown below. You may want to integrate in polar coordinates.
    🔗
    
    A closed curve \(C\) that starts at \((1,0)\text{,}\) moves in a straight line to \((2,0)\text{,}\) continues in a half circle centered at the origin with radius \(2\) in the upper half plane until the point \((-2,0)\text{,}\) then goes in a straight line to the point \((-1,0)\text{,}\) and then traverses the half circle centered at the origin with a radius of 1 located in upper half plane to end back at the point \((1,0)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    We have \(P=y^2, \ Q = 3xy\text{,}\) so \(\frac{\partial P}{\partial y} = 2y\) and \(\frac{\partial Q}{\partial x} = 3y\text{,}\) so \(\frac{\partial Q}{\partial x}-\frac{\partial P}{\partial y}=3y-2y=y\text{,}\) so then
    \begin{equation*} \oint_C y^2~dx + 3xy~dy = \iint_D y dA. \end{equation*}
    Let’s use polar coordinates, \(x=r\cos{\theta}, \ y=r\sin{\theta}\text{,}\) and \(dA = r dr d\theta\) where \({1\le r \le 2}\) and \({0\le \theta \le \pi}\text{.}\) Plugging everything in to the integral:
    \begin{align*} \iint_D y dA \amp = \int_{\theta=0}^{\pi}\int_{r=1}^2 r\sin{\theta} r dr d\theta \\ \amp = \int_{\theta=0}^{\pi} \left. \frac13 r^3 \right|_{1}^2 \sin{\theta} d\theta\\ \amp = \int_{\theta=0}^{\pi} \frac73\sin\theta d\theta \\ \amp = \left. -\frac73 \cos\theta \right|_{0}^{\pi} \\ \amp = \frac{14}{3} \end{align*}
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    3.

    Suppose that \(\ds \mbf F= (xy, \arctan y)\) and \(C\) is the simple closed curve shown below.
    🔗

    (a)

    Evaluate the line integral \(\owork{C}{F}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    
    A closed curve \(C\) that starts at \((3,0)\text{,}\) continues in a half circle centered at the origin with radius \(3\) in the upper half plane until the point \((-3,0)\text{,}\) then returns in a straight line along the x-axis to the point \((3,0)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    We have \(P=xy, \ Q = \arctan{y}\text{,}\) so \(\frac{\partial P}{\partial y} = x\) and \(\frac{\partial Q}{\partial x} = 0\text{,}\) so \(\frac{\partial Q}{\partial x}-\frac{\partial P}{\partial y}=0-x = -x.\) Then \(\owork{C}{F} = \iint_D -x dA\text{.}\) Polar coordinates would be good: \(x=r\cos{\theta}, \ y=r\sin{\theta}\text{,}\) and \(dA = r dr d\theta\) where \({0\le r \le 3}\) and \({0\le \theta \le \pi}\text{.}\) Plugging everything in to the integral:
    \begin{align*} \owork{C}{F} \amp =\int_{\theta=0}^{\pi} \int_{r=0}^3 -r\cos\theta r dr d\theta\\ \amp = \int_{\theta=0}^{\pi} \left. -\frac13 r^3 \right|_{r=0}^3 \cos\theta d \theta\\ \amp = \int_{\theta=0}^{\pi} -9\cos\theta d\theta\\ \amp = \left. -9\sin\theta\right|_0^{\pi} \\ \amp = 0. \end{align*}
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (b)

    Use your result to find the line integral \(\ds \work{C'}{F}\) where \(C'\) is just the upper semi-circle of radius 3 without the straight line along the x-axis.
    🔗
    Solution.
    The line is easier, since \(y=0\) on the line then the field simplifies to \(\mbf F = (0,0) \text{,}\) so \(\lineint{line}{F} = 0\text{.}\) Since the total work for the closed curve is 0, and the work over the line is 0, then the work over the part we want is the difference of those two. But that’s \(0-0=0\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    4.

    Each of the fields below has a constant curl. For each, state whether the curl is negative, zero, or positive.
    🔗
    Solution.
    Use Green’s Theorem here, so the curl is related to the work done around a closed curve.
    🔗
    First picture. Think of a circle centered at the origin that goes counterclockwise. Then \(\mbf F\) is always orthogonal to \(d\mbf r\text{,}\) so their dot product is always 0, so Green’s Theorem then implies the curl \(\nabla \times \mbf F = \mbf 0\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Second picture. Think of a circle centered at the origin that goes counterclockwise. Then \(\mbf F\) is always in the same direction as \(d\mbf r\text{,}\) so the work is positive, and thus the curl is positive.
    🔗
    Third picture. Think of a rectangle centered at the original and oriented up and down. Then on the left and right sides \(\mbf F\) is always orthogonal to \(d\mbf r\text{,}\) and on the top and bottom sides the work cancels out, so the work is 0 which implies the curl is 0.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    5.

    Let’s make some connections.
    🔗

    (a)

    Sketch the vector field \(\mbf F(x,y) = (y,0)\) below. (Ignore the curve already drawn for now.)
    🔗
    
    A grid to sketch a vector field on. Also included is a closed curve \(C\) in the shape of a square oriented counterclockwise through the points \((1,1), (1,-1), (3,-1), (3,1)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    Note the field gets larger in magnitude as you move away from the x-axis.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (b)

    Judging from your sketch, is the circulation (the work) around the curve positive or negative? Explain your thinking.
    🔗
    Solution.
    Think Green’s Theorem, \(\ds \oint_C \mbf F \cdot ~d\mbf r = \iint_D \nabla \times \mbf F~dA\text{,}\) but along the sides of the square we are moving orthogonal to the field, and on the top and bottom of the square we are moving against the field, so on the top and bottom we have \(\mbf F \cdot d\mbf r \lt 0\text{.}\) Since the work is negative, then the curl is also negative, so the circulation around the curve is negative.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (c)

    What about if the curve were shifted vertically?
    🔗
    Solution.
    The top field is stronger than the bottom, so even if the bottom has positive work, the top will have negative work and overpower the bottom, so we will still have negative work and thus negative curl.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (d)

    Compute \(\nabla\times\mbf F\) and explain how the curl is connected to the response to the previous two questions.
    🔗
    Solution.
    \(\nabla\times\mbf F =\frac{\partial Q}{\partial x} - \frac{\partial P}{\partial y}=-1 \text{,}\) so we’ll actually end up with
    \begin{equation*} \oint_C \mbf F \cdot d\mbf r = \iint_D -1 dA = -1(area \ of \ domain \ bounded \ by \ C) \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    6.

    Consider the field \(\ds \mbf F(x,y) = \frac12\left(-y,x\right)\) and suppose that \(C\) is a closed curve bounding a region \(D\text{.}\)
    🔗

    (a)

    Explain why \(\ds \oint_C \mbf F\cdot d\mbf r\) measures the area of \(D\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    \begin{equation*} \oint_C \mbf F\cdot d\mbf r = \iint_D\left(\frac{\partial Q}{\partial x} - \frac{\partial P}{\partial y}\right)dA = \iint_D (\frac12 - (-\frac12))dA = \iint_D 1 dA = (area \ of \ D) \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (b)

    Verify this is the case by evaluating the line integral directly where \(C\) is the circle of radius \(R\) centered at the origin.
    🔗
    Solution.
    Have to parameterize here! And not use Green’s Theorem. So we’ll take
    \begin{equation*} x = R\cos{t}, y=r\sin{t}, \end{equation*}
    then \({dx = -R\sin{t}~dt}\text{,}\) and \({dy = R\cos{t}~dt}\text{,}\) where \(t\rightarrow 0 \rightarrow 2\pi\) is the only variable and \(R\) is a constant. So the integral becomes
    \begin{align*} \oint_C \mbf F\cdot d\mbf r \amp = \oint_C -\frac12 y ~dx + \frac12 x~dy \\ \amp = \int_{0}^{2\pi} -\frac12(R\sin t)(-R\sin t)~dt + \frac12(R\cos t)(R\cos t)~dt \\ \amp = \int_0^{2\pi} \frac12 R^2 \sin^2 t + \frac12 R^2 \cos^2 t ~dt \\ \amp =\int_0^{2\pi} \frac12 R^2 ~dt\\ \amp = \frac12 R^2 (2\pi) \\ \amp = \pi R^2. \end{align*}
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    7.

    Closed curves and a field with zero curl.
    🔗

    (a)

    Suppose that \(C_1\) and \(C_2\) are two paths that begin at the same point and end at the same point and that \(\nabla\times \mbf F = 0\) in the region in between \(C_1\) and \(C_2\text{.}\) Explain why \(\ds \int_{C_1} \mbf F\cdot d\mbf r = \int_{C_2} \mbf F\cdot d\mbf r\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    Since they begin and end at the same point, then we can travel around \(C_1\) and then backwards along \(C_2\) and have a closed curve. Then according to Green’s Theorem
    \begin{equation*} \oint_{C_1 - C_2} \mbf F \cdot d\mbf r = \iint_D \nabla \times \mbf F dA = 0 \end{equation*}
    since the curl is 0 inside this region. Then we can break apart
    \begin{equation*} \oint_{C_1 - C_2} \mbf F \cdot d\mbf r = \oint_{C_1} \mbf F \cdot d\mbf r - \oint_{C_2} \mbf F \cdot d\mbf r \end{equation*}
    so setting it equal to 0 gives
    \begin{equation*} \oint_{C_1} \mbf F \cdot d\mbf r = \oint_{C_2} \mbf F \cdot d\mbf r. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (b)

    If \(C\) is a closed curve and \(\nabla \times \mbf F = 0\) in the region \(D\) bounded by \(C\text{,}\) explain why \(\ds \oint_C \mbf F \cdot d\mbf r = 0\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    By Green’s Theorem!
    \begin{equation*} \oint_{C} \mbf F \cdot d\mbf r = \iint_D \nabla \times \mbf F dA = \iint_D 0 dA = 0. \end{equation*}
