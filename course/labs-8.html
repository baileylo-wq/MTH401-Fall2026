    Method of Characteristics.

    A technique for solving specific PDEs, reducing a PDE to to a family of ODEs which can then be solved along what are called “characteristic curves” that we can then transform into a solution of the original PDE.

    \begin{gather*} \frac{dN}{dt} = (\rho v)|_{x=a} - (\rho v)|_{x = b} = -\left((\rho v)|_{x=b} - (\rho v)|_{x = a}\right) = \fillinmath{XXXXXXXXXXXX} \end{gather*}

    \begin{gather*} \frac{dN}{dt} = \int_a^b \frac{\partial \rho}{\partial t} \,dx = - \int_a^b \frac{\partial (\rho v)}{\partial x} \,dx. \end{gather*}

    Ok, we have an equation for \(v\text{.}\) Let’s plug that in to our PDE. We now have:
    \begin{gather*} \frac{\partial \rho}{\partial t} + \frac{\partial (c(1-\rho)\rho)}{\partial x} = 0. \end{gather*}

      \begin{equation*} \pd{\rho}{t} + \frac{dx}{dt}\pd{\rho}{x} = 0. \end{equation*}

    \begin{gather*} \frac{dx}{dt} = \fillinmath{XXXXXXXXXXXXXXXX} \end{gather*}

    Exploration 5. Shock Wave Example.

    Well that’s cool and all, but I did promise you shock waves. Consider the following initial density curve, \(\rho(x,0)\text{,}\) where now our curve is increasing. Just like before, draw in arrows on the density curve to indicate direction, and on the right draw characteristics.
    🔗
    Let enough time pass, what happens? A discontinuity! A shock wave! We can draw this density curve where the discontinuity will become a vertical line:
    🔗
    What does this situation correspond to in real life?
    🔗
    On the bottom of the discontinuity we have some density \(\rho_1\text{,}\) and at the top we have some density \(\rho_2\text{.}\) The shock wave moves with a speed that is the average of the speeds on either side of the shock wave. So the speed of the shock wave is
    \begin{equation*} \frac{c(1-2\rho_1)+c(1-2\rho_2)}{2} = c-(\rho_1+\rho_2) \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗

    Exploration 6. Shear Rule + Shock Waves.

    We can combine both situations to understand what happens when a traffic light changes from green to red.
    🔗
    Suppose the density is initial constant (say around \(1/4\)) with a green light, so traffic is moving smoothly (left picture). when suddenly the light turns red. Traffic past the light will keep going and clear out, but cars will pile up before the light (right picture).
    🔗
    \(\rightarrow\)
    🔗
    Now let’s turn the light green again. The Shear Rule will apply to the second shock wave where traffic is piled up, and the traffic (aka the shock wave) will begin to move to the right through the light, as pictured below.
    🔗
    \(\rightarrow\)
    🔗
    If we turn the light red before the shock wave clears, we have reached a badly timed traffic light. Thus, this model can help us time the traffic lights so the shock clears the stop light.
    🔗
    🔗
    See the article by Dr. David Austin for more details about the timing of stop lights, and for animated pictures: You’re in for a Shock.
