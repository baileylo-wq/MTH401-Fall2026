    Worksheet 1.6. Finding potentials
    

    MTH 401
    🔗

    Theorem 18.

    If \(\mbf F\) is a conservative vector field (i.e. if \(\mbf F = \nabla f\) for some \(f\) ), then
    \begin{equation*} \nabla \times \mbf F = \nabla \times (\nabla f) = \zerovec\text{.} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    However, the reverse is not always true without an additional condition!
    🔗

    Theorem 19.

    If \(\nabla \times \mbf F = \zerovec\) in a simply connected domain \(D\text{,}\) then \(\mbf F\) is a conservative field within \(D\) and we can find a potential function in \(D\) such that \(\mbf F = \nabla f\) within the domain \(D\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    To find the potential function \(f\), choose an arbitrary basepoint \(*\) and integrate over any path from that basepoint to some point \((x_0,y_0)\text{.}\) Then the potential function is
    \begin{equation*} f(x_0,y_0)= \work{C}{F}\text{.} \end{equation*}
    🔗

    1.

    Let’s consider the field \(\mbf F(x,y) = (2xy,x^2)\text{.}\)
    🔗

    (a)

    Verify that \(\nabla\times \mbf F = 0\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    \begin{equation*} \nabla\times \mbf F = \frac{\partial}{\partial x}(x^2) - \frac{\partial}{\partial y}(2xy) = 2x-2x=0. \end{equation*}
    Check.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (b)

    Suppose that \(C_1\) and \(C_2\) are two paths with the same initial and final points, explain why \(\ds \work{C_1}{F} = \work{C_2}{F}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    Consider \(\oint_{C_1-C_2}\mbf F \cdot d\mbf r = \iint_D \nabla\times \mbf F ~dA = 0\) since we have a simply connected domain and the curl is 0. (\(D\) is the interior.) That implies \(\oint_{C_1}\mbf F \cdot d\mbf r - \oint_{C_2}\mbf F \cdot d\mbf r =0\) and thus
    \begin{equation*} \oint_{C_1}\mbf F \cdot d\mbf r = \oint_{C_2}\mbf F \cdot d\mbf r. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (c)

    Let’s find a potential function for \(F\) by choosing the basepoint \(* = (0,0)\text{.}\) For a point \((x_0, y_0)\text{,}\) integrate over this curve \(C\) to define
    \begin{equation*} \ds f(x_0,y_0) = \work{C}{F}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    
    The path begins at the origin, travels along the x-axis until some value \(x_0\text{,}\) then travels vertically until it reaches the point \((x_0,y_0)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    Note that \(\ds f(x_0,y_0) = \work{C}{F} = \int_C 2xy ~dx + x^2 ~dy\text{.}\) If \(C_1\) is the horizontal part, then we can parameterize with \(x=x, y=0\) as \(x\rightarrow 0 \rightarrow x_0\text{,}\) and the integral is 0.
    🔗
    If \(C_2\) is the vertical part of the curve, we can parameterize \(x=x_0, y=y\) as \(y\rightarrow 0 \rightarrow y_0\text{,}\) so plugging in \(\work{C_2}{F} = \int_0^{y_0} 0 + x_0^2~dy = x_0^2 y_0\text{.}\) Then adding them together,
    \begin{equation*} f(x_0,y_0) = 0 + x_0^2 y_0. \end{equation*}
    (or \(f(x,y)=x^2y\text{.}\))
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (d)

    Verify that the function \(f(x,y)\) that you have found is a potential for \(\mbf F\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    \(\nabla f(x,y) = (2xy,x^2) = \mbf F\text{.}\) Check.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    2.

    Consider the field
    \begin{equation*} \ds \mbf F(x,y) = \left(-\frac{y}{x^2+y^2}, \frac{x}{x^2+y^2}\right). \end{equation*}
    We have seen that \(\nabla\times \mbf F = 0\) but that the field is not defined at the origin \((0,0)\) so that the domain of the field is not simply connected.
    🔗

    (a)

    Let’s attempt to find a potential function by integrating. Choose the basepoint to be \((1,0)\text{.}\) To define \(\ds f(x_0, y_0)\text{,}\) integrate along the positive \(x\)-axis and then along a circular arc traversed in the counterclockwise direction. (Polar coordinates could be useful for part of this...)
    🔗
    
    The path begins at the a basepoint \((1,0)\text{,}\) travels right along the \(x\)-axis for some time, then travels along a circular arc centered at the origin until it reaches the point \((x_0,y_0)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    Note first that \(\work{C}{F} = \int_C \frac{-y}{x^2+y^2}~dx + \frac{x}{x^2+y^2}~dy\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Parameterize \(C_1\text{,}\) the horizontal piece, as \(x=x, y=0\text{.}\) Then \(\work{C_1}{F} = \int 0+0 = 0\text{.}\)
    🔗
    If \(C_2\) is the curved arc with radius some constant \(R\) and the angle from the origin to our point is some constant \(\theta_0\text{,}\) we can parameterize \(x=R\cos \theta\) and \(y=R\sin \theta\) where \(\theta \rightarrow 0 \rightarrow \theta_0\text{.}\) Then \(dx = -R\sin \theta ~d\theta\) and \(dy=R\cos \theta ~d\theta\text{.}\) So the integral becomes
    \begin{align*} \work{C_2}{F} \amp = \int_{\theta=0}^{\theta_0} \frac{-R\sin \theta (-R\sin \theta)}{R^2\cos^2\theta + R^2\sin^2\theta}~d\theta + \frac{R\cos\theta (R\cos\theta)}{R^2\cos^2\theta + R^2\sin^2\theta}~d\theta \\ \amp = \int_{\theta=0}^{\theta_0} \frac{R^2\sin^2\theta}{R^2} + \frac{R^2\cos^2\theta}{R^2}~d\theta \\ \amp = \int_{\theta=0}^{\theta_0} 1 ~d\theta \\ \amp = \theta_0 \end{align*}
    So if we think of \(f\) in terms of polar coordinates, we would have \(f(r,\theta) = \theta\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (b)

    What do you find for \(f(2,0)\text{?}\) \(f(0,2)\text{?}\) \(f(-2,0)\text{?}\) \(f(0,-2)\text{?}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    At \((2,0)\text{,}\) the angle \(\theta = 0\text{,}\) or \(2\pi, -2\pi, 4\pi, \ldots\text{,}\) so \(f(2,0)\) would depend which angle you call \(\theta\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Similarly, \(f(0,2)\) could be considered \(\theta=\frac{\pi}{2}\) or...
    🔗
    You could consider \((-2,0)\) to be \(\theta=\pi\) (or ...)
    🔗
    And \(f(0,-2)\) could be \(\theta=\frac{3\pi}{2}\) (or...)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (c)

    What is a general expression for \(f(x_0, y_0)\text{?}\) It may be helpful to think in terms of polar coordinates.
    🔗
    Solution.
    Let’s keep \(f = \theta\text{!}\) Technically speaking, \(\theta = \arctan(\frac{y_0}{x_0})\text{,}\) where \(x_0^2+y_0^2 = R^2\text{...}\) but polar coordinates is simpler here.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (d)

    Why doesn’t \(f\) define a potential function for \(\mbf F\) in the region where \(\mbf F\) is defined?
    🔗
    Solution.
    It’s not a function. One input such as the point \((2,0)\) has many possible outputs because \(\theta\) has infinitely many choices that correspond to that point.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (e)

    Hmmm... Let’s try to avoid the problem (the origin) by literally going around the problem. If \(C\) is the curve shown below, what is \(\work{C}{F}\text{?}\)
    🔗
    
    The path begins at a point on the positive \(x\)-axis, travels right along the \(x\)-axis for some time, then travels counterclockwise along a circular arc centered at the origin until it almost goes full circle, then it cuts left until just to the left and below the initial starting point on the positive \(x\)-axis, then travels clockwise along a circular arc centered at the origin until it connects back with the initial point.
    🔗
    Solution.
    \(\work{C}{F} = \iint_D \nabla \times \mbf F ~dA = 0\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (f)

    Now imagine the two radial pieces of \(C\) merging together and explain why \(\ds \work{C_1}{F} = \work{C_2}{F}\) where \(C_1\) and \(C_2\) are the two circles below.
    🔗
    
    Two circles are centered at the origin and oriented counterclockwise, with \(C_1\) the circle with a smaller radius and \(C_2\) the larger radius.
    🔗
    Solution.
    We have a \(C_3\) along the connected section on the x-axis on the right. Then we can treat it as a closed curve similarly to above, where
    \begin{equation*} \oint_{C_1+C_3-C_2-C_3} \mbf F \cdot d\mbf r = \iint_D \nabla\times \mbf F~dA = 0. \end{equation*}
    (Along the connected section, \(C_3\) and \(-C_3\text{,}\) the work cancels out.). So we can simplify to
    \begin{equation*} \oint_{C_1-C_2} \mbf F \cdot d\mbf r = 0, \end{equation*}
    which implies \(\oint_{C_1} \mbf F \cdot d\mbf r=\oint_{C_2} \mbf F \cdot d\mbf r\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (g)

    If \(C_1\) and \(C_2\) are the two curves shown below, explain why \(\ds \work{C_1}{F} = \work{C_2}{F}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    Same idea, draw in a connected section that you travel in both directions to turn this into one closed curve. The connected section cancels itself out, and we have the same argument as above.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (h)

    Find \(\ds \work{C}{F}\) if \(C\) is the curve below.
    🔗
    Solution.
    We know from previous work that \(\oint_{unit \ circle} \mbf F \cdot d\mbf r = 2\pi\text{.}\) We can separate out this picture as 3 different squiggly "circles", all having the same work as the work over the unit circle. (The points where the curve crosses itself becomes where the squiggle "circles" separate.) Then we add them all up,
    \begin{equation*} \oint_C \mbf F \cdot d\mbf r = 2\pi + 2\pi + 2\pi = 6\pi. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
