    Worksheet 1.6. Finding potentials
    

    MTH 401

    Theorem 10.

    If \(\mbf F\) is a conservative vector field (i.e. if \(\mbf F = \nabla f\) for some \(f\)), then
    \begin{equation*} \nabla \times \mbf F = \nabla \times (\nabla f) = \zerovec\text{.} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    However, the reverse is not always true without an additional condition!

    Theorem 11.

    If \(\nabla \times \mbf F = \zerovec\) in a simply connected domain \(D\text{,}\) then \(\mbf F\) is a conservative field within \(D\) and we can find a potential function in \(D\) such that \(\mbf F = \nabla f\) within the domain \(D\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    To find the potential function \(f\), choose an arbitrary basepoint \(*\) and integrate over any path from that basepoint to some point \((x_0,y_0)\text{.}\) Then the potential function is
    \begin{equation*} f(x_0,y_0)= \work{C}{F}\text{.} \end{equation*}
    🔗

    1.

    Let’s consider the field \(\mbf F(x,y) = (2xy,x^2)\text{.}\)
    🔗

    (a)

    Verify that \(\nabla\times \mbf F = 0\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    (b)

    Suppose that \(C_1\) and \(C_2\) are two paths with the same initial and final points, explain why \(\ds \work{C_1}{F} = \work{C_2}{F}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    (c)

    Let’s find a potential function for \(F\) by choosing the basepoint \(* = (0,0)\text{.}\) For a point \((x_0, y_0)\text{,}\) integrate over this curve \(C\) to define
    \begin{equation*} \ds f(x_0,y_0) = \work{C}{F}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    
    The path begins at the origin, travels along the x-axis until some value \(x_0\text{,}\) then travels vertically until it reaches the point \((x_0,y_0)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    (d)

    Verify that the function \(f(x,y)\) that you have found is a potential for \(\mbf F\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    2.

    Consider the field
    \begin{equation*} \ds \mbf F(x,y) = \left(-\frac{y}{x^2+y^2}, \frac{x}{x^2+y^2}\right). \end{equation*}
    We have seen that \(\nabla\times \mbf F = 0\) but that the field is not defined at the origin \((0,0)\) so that the domain of the field is not simply connected.
    🔗

    (a)

    Let’s attempt to find a potential function by integrating. Choose the basepoint to be \((1,0)\text{.}\) To define \(\ds f(x_0, y_0)\text{,}\) integrate along the positive \(x\)-axis and then along a circular arc traversed in the counterclockwise direction. (Polar coordinates could be useful for part of this...)
    🔗
    
    The path begins at the a basepoint \((1,0)\text{,}\) travels right along the \(x\)-axis for some time, then travels along a circular arc centered at the origin until it reaches the point \((x_0,y_0)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    (b)

    What do you find for \(f(2,0)\text{?}\) \(f(0,2)\text{?}\) \(f(-2,0)\text{?}\) \(f(0,-2)\text{?}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    (c)

    What is a general expression for \(f(x_0, y_0)\text{?}\) It may be helpful to think in terms of polar coordinates.
    🔗
    🔗

    (d)

    Why doesn’t \(f\) define a potential function for \(\mbf F\) in the region where \(\mbf F\) is defined?
    🔗
    🔗

    (e)

    Hmmm... Let’s try to avoid the problem (the origin) by literally going around the problem. If \(C\) is the curve shown below, what is \(\work{C}{F}\text{?}\)
    🔗
    
    The path begins at a point on the positive \(x\)-axis, travels right along the \(x\)-axis for some time, then travels counterclockwise along a circular arc centered at the origin until it almost goes full circle, then it cuts left until just to the left and below the initial starting point on the positive \(x\)-axis, then travels clockwise along a circular arc centered at the origin until it connects back with the initial point.
    🔗
    🔗

    (f)

    Now imagine the two radial pieces of \(C\) merging together and explain why \(\ds \work{C_1}{F} = \work{C_2}{F}\) where \(C_1\) and \(C_2\) are the two circles below.
    🔗
    
    Two circles are centered at the origin and oriented counterclockwise, with \(C_1\) the circle with a smaller radius and \(C_2\) the larger radius.
    🔗
    🔗

    (g)

    If \(C_1\) and \(C_2\) are the two curves shown below, explain why \(\ds \work{C_1}{F} = \work{C_2}{F}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    (h)

    Find \(\ds \work{C}{F}\) if \(C\) is the curve below.
