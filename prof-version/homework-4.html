    MTH 401
    🔗
    Due: Monday, September 28 at the beginning of class. You will turn in a paper copy!
    🔗
    Instructions:
    🔗
    Homework submissions should represent your very best work. Your work should be neat, clearly presented and complete. In particular, include complete sentences, where appropriate, to explain your thinking. Include the problems in the order given below and be sure to allow plenty of room for your work. You will generally be marked on the methods used to solve a problem rather than just the final answer; answers alone will receive little credit.
    🔗

    1.

    Evaluate \(\oint_C xy~dx + x^2~dy\) where \(C\) is the curve shown and oriented in the counterclockwise direction.
    🔗
    🔗

    2.

    Use Green’s theorem to evaluate \(\ds \int_C e^x\cos y~dx - e^x\sin y~dy\) where \(C\) is the curve composed of two straight line segments from \(A(\ln 2, 0)\) to \(B(0,1)\) and from \(B(0,1)\) to \(C(-\ln 2,0)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    3.

    Suppose that \(C\) is a simple closed curve in the plane that bounds the region \(D\text{.}\)
    🔗

    (a)

    Explain why the integral
    \begin{equation*} \frac12\oint_C -y~dx+ x~dy = \mbox{Area}(D). \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗

    (b)

    Suppose that \(C\) is the line segment from \((x_1,y_1)\) to \((x_2,y_2)\text{.}\) Show that
    \begin{equation*} \int_C -y~dx + x~dy = x_1y_2 - x_2y_1 = \left|\begin{array}{cc} x_1 \amp x_2 \\ y_1 \amp y_2 \end{array}\right|. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗

    (c)

    Suppose that \(P\) is a polygon in the plane whose vertices, traversed in counterclockwise order, are \((x_1,y_1)\text{,}\) \((x_2,y_2)\text{,}\) ... \((x_n,y_n)\text{.}\) Show (hint use part b) that the area of \(P\) is
    \begin{equation*} \frac12\left[(x_1y_2-x_2y_1) + (x_2y_3-x_3y_2) + \ldots + (x_{n-1}y_n-x_ny_{n-1}) + (x_ny_1-x_1y_n)\right]. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗

    (d)

    Sketch the pentagon whose vertices are \((0,0)\text{,}\) \((2,1)\text{,}\) \((1,3)\text{,}\) \((0,2)\) and \((-1,0)\) and find its area. (Hint use part c.)
    🔗
    🔗

    (e)

    An ellipse whose semi-axes have length \(a\) and \(b\) may be parametrized by \(x=a\cos t\) and \(y=b\sin t\) where \(0\le t\le 2\pi\text{.}\) Show that this ellipse has area \(\pi ab\text{.}\) (Hint use part a.)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    4.

    Let’s explore a different identity.
    🔗

    (a)

    Show Green’s first identity, assuming the usual notation: \(C\) is a simple closed curve and \(D\) is the region it bounds:
    \begin{equation*} \oint_C f(\nabla g)\cdot \mbf n~ds = \iint_D f\nabla^2 g ~dA + \iint_D \nabla f \cdot \nabla g ~dA. \end{equation*}
    You will use this identity in parts (b) and (c). Hint: Recall from homework 2 you proved an identity that may be very helpful here.
    🔗
    🔗

    (b)

    A harmonic function \(g\) is one for which \(\nabla^2 g = 0\text{.}\) Using part (a) and choosing \(f\) very carefully, show that, if \(g\) is harmonic then
    \begin{equation*} \oint_C \nabla g \cdot \mbf n~ ds = 0. \end{equation*}
    We will see later that the steady-state temperature distribution in a region is a harmonic function. We’ll also see that the fact above shows the intuitively obvious fact that, in the steady-state, the net flow of heat into the region must be zero.
    🔗
    🔗

    (c)

    Suppose that \(f\) is harmonic and \(f(x,y) = 0\) on the curve \(C\text{.}\) Show that
    \begin{equation*} \iint_D|\nabla f|^2~dA = 0. \end{equation*}
    and explain why this means that \(f(x,y) = 0\) at every point inside the region \(D\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Again, this makes intuitive sense: if the steady-state temperature is constantly zero on the boundary of a region, we should expect that the temperature is zero at every point inside the region.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    5.

    Consider the vector field \(\mbf F = (y, x-3)\text{.}\)
    🔗

    (a)

    Verify that \(\nabla\times \mbf F = 0\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    (b)

    After choosing the basepoint \(*=(0,0)\text{,}\) create a potential function \(f(x,y)\) by integrating from the basepoint to a general point \((x,y)\text{.}\) (You can probably guess what \(f\) is, but I would like for you to find it by integrating.)
    🔗
    🔗

    (c)

    If one of your classmates were to choose a different basepoint, how would your two functions differ?
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    6.

    Suppose we have an infinitely long electrically charged cylindrical pipe whose charge density is \(\rho\) Coulombs per cubic meter. That is, supposed we have a solid cylinder with charge equally distributed throughout the whole cylinder. Assume \(\rho\) is positive (electric field points away from positive charge and towards negative charge), and assume the radius of the cylinder is \(R\) (and just to emphasize, the cylinder is not hollow).
    🔗

    (a)

    Find the electric field \(\vec{E}\) outside the cylindrical solid. (What would be a useful choice for the Gaussian surface?)
    🔗
    🔗

    (b)

    Find the electric field \(\vec E\) inside the cylindrical solid. (What would be a useful choice for the Gaussian surface?)
    🔗
    🔗

    (c)

    Find a continuous potential function \(f\) so that \(\vec{E} = \nabla f\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Note: You will want to create the potential by integrating so that the potential you find is continuous. What may be a good basepoint?
    🔗
